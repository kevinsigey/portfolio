An energy forensic

The Hidden Megawatts

A forensic reading of aggregate ZIP-level electricity use—a data-center proxy, not a facility meter—and what its anomalies may reveal about the AI boom.

BDI 513 Group 10 · Yun-Chuan Ho · Hamza Khan · Yu-Lan Liu · Prayag Patil · Kevin Sigey · Majesty Umoye · University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

The reporting blind spot

A quiet ZIP code with an impossible pulse.

Most months, downtown San Jose ZIP 95113—the project’s data-center proxy—traces a familiar commercial rhythm. Then its aggregate series collapses below one million kilowatt-hours or vaults beyond 200 million.

That volatility could reflect physical demand, changing customer composition, or reporting treatment. Under CPUC Decision 14-05-016, PG&E aggregates usage before publishing it: at least 100 residential or 15 non-residential customers per ZIP, with no single customer contributing more than 15% of the total. When thresholds are not met, published geography may be suppressed or combined.

The project tests a load-masking hypothesis: an abrupt ZIP-level collapse paired with an offsetting neighbor surge may indicate that aggregated load moved across a reporting boundary. The public rows cannot identify a facility, customer, meter, or workload.

The project’s broader neighborhood analysis reported 23 reallocations. The two-ZIP extract used in this paper is narrower: its live trailing-median screen flags the six sharp 95113 collapses visible here. That distinction matters—the chart demonstrates the signal, while the 23-count belongs to the full project pipeline.

Methodology

From public utility rows to a forensic series.

The analysis combines PG&E public ZIP-code usage across commercial, residential, and industrial classes with a rule-based masking detector, then compares month-over-month and year-over-year movement.

Establish the ZIP baselineCompute a robust median for each geography rather than letting peaks set the norm.
Find focal collapsesFlag months where 95113 falls below 50% of its trailing 12-month median.
Search adjacent ZIPsCheck neighbors for contemporaneous usage above 150% of their own baseline.
Reallocate cautiouslyPair low/high anomalies only where timing and magnitude support a likely reporting shift.
Validate the patternRecompute month-over-month, year-over-year, phase averages, and extreme months.

Detector shown in this page. For month t, the focal ZIP is flagged when Usage95113(t) < 0.5 × median(Usage95113(t−12 … t−1)). A minimum of six prior observations is required. The browser computes this from the embedded CSV rows; it does not fetch data.

Full-project reallocation. The broader source analysis also searched neighboring ZIPs for an offsetting increase above 150% of their baseline and reported 23 reallocations. The supplied two-ZIP extract cannot reproduce the neighbor search, so this paper does not pretend that it can.

Transformations. Month-over-month change compares adjacent observations. Year-over-year change compares the same calendar month 12 observations apart. No interpolation is used; the source period ends in September 2025.

Limitations. ZIP aggregation prevents facility-level attribution. Event timing creates hypotheses, not causal proof. Meter reclassification, reporting boundaries, weather, occupancy, and other large customers can also move the series.

What it means

Grid planners see the average.
Aggregate peaks arrive as shocks.

Apparent demand can be a floor

The project interprets masked periods as evidence that AI-related loads may be two to three times what a single raw ZIP series suggests. This is a scenario range from the project—not a facility-level causal estimate.

For planning, the key risk is volatility: a network designed around a calm monthly average can still face sharp, concentrated ramps. Facility-level evidence would be needed to determine whether any given ramp came from model training, inference, or another large load.
