Is Anthropic worth $2T? What the price requires, and where we think the stock trades

A $2T price, about $118 a share, is a bet that three things all go right: Anthropic holds its enterprise lead, moves more of its revenue up from metered tokens into applications and agents, and keeps the owners of compute from taking the margin. We put the odds of all three at about 15% [our judgment]. Weighting every outcome by its odds gives $98 a share, or $1.66T [modeled], and we expect the stock to trade between $85 and $110, or $1.44T to $1.86T. A $2T print is reachable as a first-day close. It is not our estimate of fair value.

Executive summary

The answer: $2T is a first-day number, not a fair value. It is about $118 a share on roughly 16.9B fully diluted shares derived from the filing. At 10 times sales it needs $200B of revenue, 3.1 times today's $65B run rate (Bloomberg, July 2026; press-reported, the S-1 leaves its run-rate field blank). At the high-growth peer multiple of 20.9 times it needs only $96B, which is why the price is not absurd: it asks revenue to grow into it while the multiple holds.

We weight four outcomes by our odds and get $98 a share, with a median nearer $78. A $2T expected value would need the best outcome to have about a 25% chance, not our 15%. We expect the stock to price near the $1.5T filing-day talk, about $89, and trade between $85 and $110. Large IPOs above $50B have shown a median first-day gain of about 25%, which takes $1.5T to about $1.9T, so a $2T close on day one is plausible. Cerebras and Figma now trade below their offer prices and SpaceX dipped below its offer in July, so a first-day value is not valuation evidence, and that is why we do not carry $2T as the twelve-month value. Precedent transactions pull the other way, and they are the main reason our trading band reaches above the multiples center. Google paid $32B for Wiz, about 64 times trailing ARR and about 32 times forward ARR, and about 40% more than the $23B Wiz had rejected a year earlier. Meta paid $14.3B for 49% of Scale AI, which implies more than $29B for the whole company, or about 33 times 2024 revenue. Against those, Salesforce paid about $8B for Informatica at roughly 4.6 times ARR, and about $5.7B for Braze in October 2026 at roughly 6 times projected sales and a 58% premium to the prior close. The pattern is consistent. Full control of revenue that already exists costs a public multiple plus a control premium of roughly 30 to 80%. Scarcity and talent deals price per team and per capability, with revenue as decoration. OpenAI's io purchase, about $6.5B for a 55-person team with no revenue, and the Character.AI and Inflection acqui-hires have no meaningful revenue multiple at all. Applied to Anthropic with a scale discount at the low end, the usable precedents give $58 to $123 a share. The top of that range is an auction price, and one of that deal class's leftovers, Character.AI, filed for Chapter 11 in October 2026. The precedent row is therefore carried in §1 as an upper bound, not as a central case.

§3

The business: revenue is compounding and compute cost is falling

Three charts from the S-1 explain the whole debate: how fast revenue grew, how fast cost fell, and where each revenue dollar goes. Revenue grew about 112× from FY2023 to FY2025. Compute cost fell from more than ten times revenue to under half of it, mostly because the denominator exploded.

Anthropic is not a product that might one day find customers: revenue has compounded at a pace almost no public software company has sustained, and Q2 2026 brought its first operating profit, +$0.97B or an 8.4% margin (S-1, Oct 26, 2026). The other half of the filed story is cost. Compute dwarfed revenue for most of the company’s life, and the filing warns that contracted step-ups will push costs back up. Growth and cost are racing, and the valuation depends on which keeps its lead.

Quarterly revenue ($B)

Revenue rose from $0.38B in Q1 2025 to $11.6B in Q2 2026, about 31 times in five quarters (S-1, quarterly statements). Annual: FY2023 $0.04B, FY2024 $0.39B, FY2025 $4.59B, H1 2026 $16.29B. The $65B run rate (Bloomberg, July 2026) is press-reported and is not plotted, because the S-1 leaves its Run-Rate field blank.

Compute cost and operating margin, % of revenue

Compute and infrastructure fell from 251% of revenue to 48% in six quarters while operating margin swung from −249% to +8% (S-1). Compute here includes research and training compute, not only serving cost. Warning inside the filing: contracted step-ups (SpaceXAI alone about $9.3B a quarter at full delivery from Dec 2026) can push the ratio back up in 2027.

Where H1 2026 revenue went ($B)

Revenue $16.29B less cost of revenue $2.38B, research and development $9.74B, sales and marketing $3.81B and general and administrative $1.05B leaves an operating loss of $0.69B (S-1, supplemental expense table; the Q2 profit offset a Q1 loss). Research and development is mostly training compute, which is why the headline compute ratio is far above the serving cost. H1 net loss was $80.7B, of which $72.4B is a non-cash change in the fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs.

Anthropic's pricing already reflects pressure from both directions. At the top, its flagship models still command a premium that rivals have not yet matched away. At the bottom, prices have converged so completely that the cheapest credible Chinese model is no longer cheaper than Anthropic's own cheapest model on a like-for-like basis. The competition has moved from the price of a token to the cost of completing a whole task.

What the internet boom teaches about who ends up on top

The last time a technology was priced like this, the technology won and most of the pioneers did not. The survivors shared two things: distribution they owned, and customers and cash that did not depend on the hype asset itself. The financials below show which company had which.

The internet boom is the closest precedent for who ends up owning the customer. It ran the sequence AI is running now: a transformative technology, capital pricing the future as if it had already arrived, a build-out far ahead of demand, and a crash that destroyed most of the pioneers while leaving their infrastructure behind for the survivors. The survivors were sorted by two things: distribution they owned, and a cash machine that did not depend on the hype asset itself.

How the winners actually won

Microsoft

Its Windows and Office license cash never depended on internet revenue, so the crash was a share price event, not a solvency event. It gave Internet Explorer away inside Windows, and Netscape, whose 1995 IPO started the boom, was sold to AOL in 1998 and faded. Azure is now the second-largest cloud, and Microsoft holds about 27% of OpenAI after its October 2025 restructuring, on roughly $13B invested.

Google

The one pure play that won. It was profitable from 2001 because AdWords charged only per click. It rented distribution inside its rivals first, powering search for Yahoo in 2000 and AOL in 2002, then bought the next era's distribution: Android for about $50M in 2005, Chrome in 2008, and the Safari default for about $20B a year. It listed in August 2004 at $85 a share, about $23B, after the bust had cleared the field.

The losers Yahoo peaked at about $125B in January 2000 and sold its core business for $4.48B in 2017, with its Alibaba and Yahoo Japan stakes kept by shareholders in Altaba. Pets.com raised $82.5M, reached roughly $290M to $300M on $619K of revenue, and liquidated about 268 days after its IPO.

  • A cash machine that does not depend on the hype asset. Google had one from 2001. Microsoft's sat outside the internet entirely.
  • Distribution the winner controls. Google rented it inside its rivals, then bought it. Microsoft already owned it on the desktop.
  • A balance sheet that lasts through the crash. Google raised public money after the bust, into a cleared field. Microsoft never needed the market's permission to survive.

Every hype wave ends with fewer owners

Every wave in this paper's evidence consolidated around distribution plus funding endurance, not around the best technology or the first mover. Carlota Perez described the mechanism in 2002. Financial capital overbuilds during the installation phase and is impaired in the crash. In the deployment phase that follows, the survivors capture the infrastructure the crash made cheap. Railroads and airlines followed the same path. The survivors carried low leverage through the panic and owned the through-routes and hubs, which is distribution.

Why the downside is steeper than it looks: the compute commitments

Anthropic's grid is contracted compute, hundreds of billions of dollars of commitments signed ahead of the revenue that must pay for them. Customer revenue can be cancelled on 30 to 90 days' notice, while much of this stack cannot be, or only on terms that hand suppliers equity instead of cash. If growth continues, the stack looks like foresight. If it slows, the same stack is how stress turns into dilution.

The named commitments in the filing, by supplier

The stack is not one obligation type: Broadcom’s $161.2B is non-cancellable 5-yr leases; Google (≥$114.5B, to 2033) and Amazon (≥$110B, to 2036) are pay-regardless minimums; SpaceXAI (up to $120.5B) is cancellable on 90 days’ notice after initial periods ending Jan/Mar 2027. The price makes three demands at once:

Telecom's history states the bear case plainly: the carriers built the grid, and Apple ended up worth about ten times all three of them combined. The internet's history sharpens it. That is the question the IPO price asks. It is not whether intelligence becomes commonplace, because on every precedent in this report it will. At our odds the Google ending is a 15% outcome, and most of the probability sits in the middle, where Anthropic is a durable, well-valued company worth less than $2T. A2 · Three ways to build a business above the model

"Consumer AI is a power-user business wearing a mass-market costume."

Only 4.5% of US consumers pay for ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, and spending is concentrated in a small group of heavy users. The three directions below start from that reality rather than from the costume.

The public image of consumer AI is mass-market, but the economics are the opposite. A small group of power users, paying meaningful monthly sums, accounts for a disproportionate share of the money, and Anthropic’s base is the clearest example: far smaller than ChatGPT’s, and unusually heavy on its most expensive tier. If the token is heading to commodity pricing, the durable businesses sit around that base. The three avenues are deliberately distinct: deepen the consumer base, de-risk renting the grid, and protect the feedback advantage.

A

Grow the consumer business Anthropic already has

Verdict: build and buy capability, not users: the payer quality is already there (chart, right); the gap is distribution.

  • Acquire consumer capability and improve the current offering until the client base compounds. The honest starting fact: Anthropic has made zero consumer acquisitions; every deal on record is enterprise or developer infrastructure (Humanloop, Bun, Vercept, Coefficient Bio ~$400M stock, Stainless >$300M).
  • Precedents price consumer capability as talent + interface, not users: OpenAI paid ~$6.5B for io (~55 people, no users); Google paid $2.7B for a non-exclusive Character.AI license + founders; Microsoft/Inflection ~$620M.
  • The asset worth scaling is payer quality (chart, right): the gap is distribution, not willingness to pay.

No public source shows Anthropic pursuing this yet; its revealed preference is enterprise. Character.AI's Chapter 11 (filed Oct 12, 2026; $23.6M 2025 revenue vs $56.1M compute in Jan–Aug 2026, carried hedged; sourcing is thinner than other figures here) is what standalone consumer economics look like without a lab behind them.

The payer-quality gap: and the distribution gap

Claude's subscribers choose the expensive tier at ~7× ChatGPT's rate (Sensor Tower panel), while ChatGPT has 14× the mobile users and 3× the US paid subscribers. Avenue A is buying reach for a base that already pays.

B

Reduce the risk of renting the whole grid

Verdict: don’t buy the plant: minority stakes buy information, not supply. The version that fits is Broadcom-style co-design plus PPAs: a post-2030 hedge and a bargaining chip for the 2028–31 renegotiation window.

  • Nobody in the precedent set bought a power plant. Microsoft’s Three Mile Island deal is a 20-yr PPA (835 MW, restart 2027); Amazon’s $650M Talen deal bought the data-center campus, not the plant; Meta’s ~6.6 GW is PPAs and funding agreements; Google’s Kairos deal is 500 MW by 2035, first power 2030.
  • The timing doesn’t close: new nuclear delivers 2030–35; the commitments bite 2026–33. At fully-loaded campus costs, $131.7B prudently funds ~0.5–1 GW owned (15–25% of cash), a single-digit share of what’s rented.
  • The cost collapse was achieved as a renter: 1,056% → 48% of revenue, arbitraging four chip platforms. Ownership would trade that arbitrage for exposure to a single technology’s timeline (the Oklo chart, right).

Why not just buy the power? The Oklo warning

Oklo, the most direct "own the reactor" trade, carries a ~$4.9B market cap and fell ~79% in a year (monthly closes, right). An equity stake in a pre-revenue reactor company is exposure to the theme, not contracted megawatts.

C

Protect the data and feedback advantage behind the product

Verdict: protect the loop, don’t buy the vendor: the Scale AI purchase (visual, right) shows how buying the supplier destroys the neutrality that made it valuable.

  • Anthropic’s edge is customer focus. Its products are the best to use, and that quality generates the first-party feedback loop (diagram, right). The right investment secures that loop; it does not buy a vendor.
  • What Anthropic actually owns: Constitutional AI / RLAIF (in-house synthetic feedback since Dec 2022), Surge AI as reported core feedback partner ($1.2B reported 2024 revenue, bootstrapped), Mercor expert supply, and Claude writing >90% of its own codebase (Sep 2026, per S-1 summaries), "closing the loop."
  • The moat is verifiers + a fresh real-data anchor (~10% real data retained avoids model collapse, Nature, 2024) + exclusive domain-expert supply. Licensed data is a commodity (~$800M/yr disclosed, all non-exclusive); Anthropic’s largest data cash flow so far is a liability: the $1.5B Bartz settlement (approved Jul 2026).
