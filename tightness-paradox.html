Labor Markets · A Replication

The Tightness Paradox

For a decade, hard hiring did not push wages anywhere. After COVID, in the hardest-to-hire states, it started to. This paper is the road to that sentence. A class paper claimed the effect was everywhere. A rebuild on all fifty states found it nowhere. A search of industries and states found nothing either, until the sample was cut to the years after COVID, where the hardest-to-hire states split away from the rest. A test registered before it was run says the split is real. What follows is every step, including the dead ends, because the dead ends are what make the last step believable.

The rest of this paper is the evidence chain behind that one chart, in the order it happened. Section 1 states the original claim. Section 2 rebuilds it on the full country and watches it collapse. Sections 3 and 4 search the two places an effect could still hide, across industries and across states, and find nothing on the full sample. Section 5 is the anomaly the search turned up, and section 6 is the pre-registered test of it, the chart above. Section 7 asks what kind of states run high on friction, and whether being that kind of state explains the test. Section 8 states what survives. Section 9 asks whether the postings behind the headline are real. Section 10 is method, limits, and the one caveat that matters most, kept where it cannot be missed.

1 · The claim

The question came out of the post-pandemic labor market. Openings ran far above unemployment, employers said they could not hire, and a visible share of postings looked like ghost jobs: openings posted with little intent to fill them. The standard tightness measure, openings per unemployed worker, treats every posting as real demand. I wanted to know whether the postings that never convert into hires carry their own signal.

The proxy is simple. Hiring friction is unfilled openings per worker: openings minus hires, scaled by the labor force, lagged one quarter because firms adjust posted wages slowly. The original paper, written for ACCY 512 at Illinois, built a state-by-month panel of the 25 largest states from April 2015 to December 2023 and regressed log real wages on lagged friction with state and month fixed effects, standard errors clustered by state.

The claim (original sample)
Beta = 6.17, p < 0.05

A one-percentage-point rise in the friction proxy was associated with roughly 6 percent higher real wages within states over time.

The paradox
The sign flips

Across states, high-friction states pay less. Within a state over time, rising friction predicted rising wages. That reversal named the paper.

The nulls, reported then too
Two tests came back empty

Friction did not explain the conventional tightness ratio, and the friction-wage link showed no detectable break around the pandemic.

Those two nulls were in the class paper from the start, reported as faithfully as the headline. What the class paper could not do was test its own headline on data it had not selected. The 25 largest states are not a random draw of the U.S. labor market, and a coefficient estimated on them carries that choice inside it. The rebuild was the test.

2 · The collapse

The rebuild keeps the original design intact and changes one thing: the sample. Same friction formula, same real-wage construction (CES hourly earnings deflated by national CPI into January-2015 dollars), same two-way fixed-effects specification with the same controls, now on all 50 states plus DC, April 2015 to December 2025, 6,528 observations. State JOLTS is what binds the end of the panel, and the end is exact: BLS published monthly state JOLTS through December 2025 and moves the state estimates to an annual release from there, so the panel stops where the monthly series stops.

The first check was whether the rebuild even measures the same thing. On the 2,625 observations where the two panels overlap, labor force, the unemployment rate, hourly wages, and service-sector employment match the original pull exactly. Openings and hires differ slightly, by BLS vintage revisions published since the original download. The inputs agree. Whatever happens to the coefficient is not a data error.

The headline estimate: original vs rebuild specifications

Sources: original sample as reported in the ACCY 512 paper (its standard error is not carried in the rebuild files, so no interval is drawn for it); rebuild from headline_estimates.csv and robust_loso.csv, Phase 1 rebuild, September 28, 2026. The three rebuild rows are the same point estimate under three standard-error choices.

The coefficient falls from 6.17 to 0.058. That is a decline of about 99 percent in the point estimate, and the p-value rises from below 0.05 to 0.54. On the full sample, hiring friction does not predict real wages. Even taken at face value, the rebuild's average marginal effect is small: one standard deviation of lagged friction maps to roughly 0.14 percent higher real wages three months later, inside a confidence interval that comfortably includes zero and small negatives.

The original paper closed by translating its coefficient into a dollar figure, an annual inefficiency tax on employers. That exhibit depended entirely on the 6.17. The rebuild's own back-of-envelope version comes to $11.9B a year with a standard error of $15.3B, which is not an estimate but a range that includes nothing happening. The tax exhibit is cut from this paper. A magnitude built on a coefficient that does not survive its own rebuild is not a magnitude.

A coefficient that falls from 6.17 to 0.06 when the sample doubles was never a fact about the labor market. It was a fact about the sample.

3 · The search: industries

A state-level null leaves one place for the story to hide: aggregation. The industry panel is national, 10 industries by 135 months, and the friction measure is the direct one: the unfilled share, one minus hires over openings, lagged three months. If unfilled openings push wages up only where phantom postings concentrate, the effect should appear here, and strongest in the two industries picked in advance: education and health services, and information.

The industry test: per-industry estimates and the pooled result

Per-industry estimates from per_industry.csv (industry_run.py), each an OLS regression of log real wages on the lagged unfilled share with a trend. Intervals are computed as the estimate plus or minus 1.96 times its Newey-West standard error. Pooled estimate: two-way fixed effects with two-way clustered standard errors, N = 1,350.

It does not appear. Both predicted industries are null, and the estimates that do reach significance run mostly negative, the wrong sign for the hypothesis: where unfilled openings predict anything, they predict lower wages. That fits reverse causality, low-wage industries finding openings harder to fill, better than it fits tightness pushing wages up. The Driscoll-Kraay pooled p-value of 0.009 gets one honest sentence: it falls to 0.48 once industry-specific trends are added, and a positive pooled estimate assembled from negative industry estimates is a weighting artifact, not a result. The industry level is null too.

4 · The search: states

Start inside the pooled regression. Split the states six ways (by average friction, unemployment, wage level, industry mix, labor force size, and Census region), let the friction coefficient differ across each split, and test whether the pieces differ. On the full sample they do not: no split produces group coefficients that differ significantly from each other (smallest joint p-value: 0.11). Averaging across state types hides nothing.

One regression per state: the spread is real, the sign is wrong

Each row is one state's own time-series regression of log real wages on lagged friction, with the pooled model's controls plus a trend. Red marks the eight states that survive a Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery correction at 5 percent; all eight are negative, and Delaware and Oklahoma also survive a Bonferroni correction. Source: state heterogeneity exploration (state_groups_run.py), November 1, 2026; estimates in state_groups_estimates.csv.

State by state, the estimates do spread, wider than their standard errors permit (Q = 113 on 50 degrees of freedom; about 56 percent of the spread is real dispersion, not sampling noise). But the spread runs negative: 41 of 51 coefficients are below zero, and the eight states that survive the false-discovery correction (Arizona, Delaware, Florida, Hawaii, Idaho, Minnesota, North Carolina, Oklahoma) are all negative. No state shows a positive effect that survives correction. One design warning applies: a single-state regression cannot absorb the national cycle the way the pooled model's month fixed effects do, and friction is countercyclical, so each state's coefficient also reflects its wages' exposure to the national cycle, 2020 and 2021 above all. Treat the state estimates as co-movement, not as state-level effects.

That should have ended it. On the full sample, every way of cutting the states says nothing, or less than nothing. One cut did not fit that verdict, and it was not a cut by state type. It was a cut by time.

5 · The turn: the recent window

Cut the panel at COVID. From January 2022 to December 2025 (N = 2,397), the pooled coefficient is +0.102 and still not significant (p = 0.29): the recent average is as quiet as the decade's. Group the states by their average friction, though, and the coefficients climb: −0.28 in the low-friction tercile, −0.07 in the middle, +0.59 in the high tercile, where the estimate itself is significant (p = 0.008). The test that the three are equal gives p = 0.034. The same upward tilt shows up when states are grouped by unemployment, by industry mix, and by labor force size, and the high groups of those different splits overlap by only about 7 of 17 states, so it is not one cluster of states recycled through four groupings.

Three reasons not to believe it yet. First, it was one rejection among twelve equality tests run across the two windows; adjusted for the number of tests, it sits around p = 0.41, which is what luck looks like. Second, the terciles in that run were defined on full-period averages, so the recent window helped define its own groups. Third, the state-by-state estimates inside the recent window are unstable: the only two states that survive correction point in opposite directions (New Jersey at +1.49, Nevada at −1.10), state rankings between the full sample and the recent window correlate at only 0.14, and the full sample's corrected survivors fade (Delaware −0.61, p = 0.10; Oklahoma flips to +0.24). Whatever the recent window holds, it is not a stable list of states where friction works.

A coherent group pattern standing on unstable state estimates is exactly the case a pre-registered test exists for. The design was written down on November 1, 2026, before the test was run: freeze the groups on data from before the window, run one equality test, report whatever comes out. The next section is that test.

6 · The test: pre-registered

The registration fixed every choice in advance. Terciles from each state's mean friction over April 2015 to December 2019 only, seventeen states per tercile, so nothing from the test window could shape the groups. Freezing moved 12 of 51 states into a different tercile than the full-period averages had put them in. The window: January 2022 to December 2025. The specification: the rebuild's preferred one, friction interacted with the frozen terciles. The test: one joint Wald test that the three coefficients are equal, at the 5 percent level. No second test was registered.

Low-friction states (frozen tercile)
Beta = −0.19, p = 0.42

Null. Where hiring was easiest before COVID, friction and wages do not move together after it.

Mid-friction states (frozen tercile)
Beta = −0.21, p = 0.23

Null as well, and nearly identical to the low group. The split is not a gradient. It is the top tercile separating from the other two.

High-friction states (frozen tercile)
Beta = +0.65, p = 0.004

A one-percentage-point rise in the friction proxy is associated with roughly 0.65 percent higher real wages within these states after COVID: the original paper's sign and story, at about a tenth of its size.

The test rejects: chi-square 9.22 on 2 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0099. The three coefficients are not equal, and the difference is the high tercile. Run the identical frozen-group test on the full sample instead and it does not reject (p = 0.37), which is the point. The heterogeneity is not a permanent feature of high-friction states. It switched on, around the pandemic, in the states where hiring was already hardest.

So the thread from section 5 is promoted: from suggestive to tested. It is not promoted to settled, and the difference matters enough that section 10 gives it the first word. The short version: this test ran on the same window that suggested the hypothesis. Freezing the groups removed the window's say over the groups. It could not remove the window's say over the question. The deciding run needs data this paper has not seen.

7 · The search: state types

The terciles locate the effect, not what kind of state a high-friction state is, or whether kind is the effect in disguise. So the search widened: 25 published state characteristics, BEA, BLS, Census, and Labor Department series, set against each state's mean friction level and mean unfilled share. The profile is not distress. Unfilled openings concentrate where five-year real GDP growth ran slow, where more of the workforce holds a bachelor's degree, where professional and business services and education and health carry more of the economy, where work from home is common and minimum wages run high, and where leisure and hospitality and population growth are small.

Friction lives in prosperous, educated, white-collar labor markets that hire through long pipelines. Two discounts ride on the profile: the openings and hires rates are near-relatives of the target and flatter any model built on them, and a few large correlations in the full list are District of Columbia leverage. Every bar in the chart survives both checks.

Then the same 25 characteristics were set against the coefficients themselves, each state's friction beta, full sample and post-2022 window. Nothing survives. The raw candidates (professional services share, GDP growth, average pay, household income) all die under false-discovery correction, the best adjusted value near 0.20, and penalized models predict a state's beta worse than its average. So the effect is not wealth, education, industry mix, or housing costs wearing a mask. What kind of state a state is explains where friction lives. It does not explain where friction started to price.

8 · What survives

Two claims survive everything in this paper. The first is about measurement, and it is the solid one. The second is the headline, and it is younger, narrower, and one retest away from trouble. They are stated in that order because that is the order of how much weight each can carry.

Start with measurement. The wage claim is gone at the state and industry level alike. The descriptive fact underneath it is not. Unfilled openings are large, persistent, and concentrated exactly where the original intuition said phantom postings would live.

Where openings go unfilled: mean unfilled share by industry

Computed from the industry panel (industry_panel.csv), means over 135 months per industry. The two clay bars are the industries named in advance, before the test was run.

In education and health services, about 47 percent of openings do not convert into a hire in the month, and in financial activities the share is about 46 percent. Compare total private industry at about 19 percent, professional and business services at about 17 percent, and trade and transportation at about 9 percent, and two industries where the share is negative: leisure and hospitality and construction, where hires in a month routinely exceed that month's openings because hires fill postings from earlier months.

That spread is the finding. The unfilled share is not a uniform fog over the labor market; it piles up in industries built on credentialed roles, evergreen requisitions, and long hiring pipelines, the places where a posting can stay open without anyone urgently intending to fill it this month. And it can be negative where hiring is fast and churn is high. A measure that behaves this way is not a clean read on demand pressure. Openings minus hires does not behave like tightness: it did not predict wages across states, it did not predict wages across industries, and in the original paper it did not explain the conventional tightness ratio either. Three separate tests point the same way. What JOLTS counts as an opening is a mix of genuine vacancies and postings that were never going to convert on any timeline the wage data can see, and the mix differs by industry in a stable, measurable pattern.

The second survivor is the located effect. For the decade as a whole, friction did not predict wages in any kind of state. From 2022, in the third of states where hiring was hardest before COVID, it does, at +0.65 with a pre-registered test behind it. If that survives the retest on new data, the reading is that the post-pandemic labor market changed what an unfilled opening means in the places where openings pile up: labor scarce enough that a posting which stays open really does bid wages up, there and then, and not before and not elsewhere. That sentence is a hypothesis with one passed test behind it, and this paper labels it as such.

The nearest published neighbor to that reading should be named. Autor, Dube, and McGrew, in “The Unexpected Compression” (NBER Working Paper 31010, 2023), found that after the pandemic, labor market tightness became predictive of real wage growth for low-wage workers across states: where markets ran hottest, pay at the bottom rose fastest, compressing the distribution from below. Same instinct as this paper, that after COVID, geography started to matter for wages. Different variable, different wage, different design. Their variable is tightness itself, vacancies per unemployed worker. This paper's variable is conversion failure: openings that do not become hires, the vacancy yield read from the other side. A state can be tight and converting, or tight and not converting; the two rankings are not the same list. Their wage is the bottom of the distribution, moved by a job-ladder mechanism, workers climbing to better-paying employers. This paper's wage is the state mean, and if the effect in the high-friction tercile were only their channel passing through a correlated measure, it should wash out in mean wages. It does not. Whether that makes it a broader fact or an artifact is what the retest is for. And their design compares eras across states, while this one first spends a decade failing to find the effect anywhere, on the pooled sample, on six groupings, and state by state, and then runs one pre-committed equality test on groups frozen before the window opened. The overlap is real. So is the distance.

Both survivors are smaller claims than the original paper made, and both survived things the original claim did not. The paradox dissolves once the measurement and the calendar are taken seriously: measured tightness looked paradoxical because part of it was never demand, and because the part that is demand only started pricing into wages recently, in the states under the most hiring pressure.

9 · Are the postings real?

The located effect has one cheap explanation left, and the published flows can test it. If the high-friction states' unfilled openings were mostly dead postings, the stock would sit open and not convert. It converts. The stock is older: 1.64 months to clear at the current hire rate in a high-friction state, against 1.41 in a low-friction one. Within states, a larger openings stock predicts more hires the next quarter in the high tercile (+0.28, p < 0.001), the same as in the other terciles, up from +0.18 in the same states before COVID, and positive in 16 of its 17 states.

Older stock, normal conversion: the frozen terciles since 2022

Education and health, the highest-unfilled industry, shows the same shape: duration lengthened from 1.74 months before COVID to 2.15 after, pipeline positive throughout. Two things this check cannot do. It cannot price a phantom layer of the size the intent literature estimates, about a fifth of postings; a layer that size hides inside a converting stock, and pricing it waits on posting-level duration data that are not public. And it cannot separate the third kind of posting, continuous-recruitment openings, genuinely open and genuinely slow, from either of the other two. What it rules out is the crude version: the cells carrying the headline do not hold a stock of postings that never hire.

10 · Method and limits

First, the caveat the headline owes, stated as plainly as the headline. The pre-registered test in section 6 was registered and run on November 1, 2026, on data through December 2025: the same window whose exploratory split suggested the hypothesis. Registration froze the groups, the window, the specification, and the single test, so the result is not the product of trying cuts until one worked. What registration on the same data cannot do is make the window new. The honest status is tested, not settled. The settling test is already specified, and it has to live with a data constraint. The monthly state JOLTS release ended with the December 2025 data; the state estimates, which are model-based and revisable in any case, are annual from there. Confirmation therefore runs on two tracks: the identical design on each annual state JOLTS vintage as it publishes, and a division-level version of the same test on monthly Census division JOLTS, which still publishes monthly, for the forward window. Neither track is the clean monthly state rerun this design would have had a few years earlier. This page will carry the results either way. The frozen design, the group assignments, and the downgrade rule are registered publicly as The Vintage Test, timestamped November 1, 2026, before any forward data exist.

The rebuild's data choices are documented in its files, and the soft spots are these. State minimum wages before 2019 and education attainment at the sample edges are filled from the nearest observed years, because the cited sources start later; a specification dropping the minimum-wage control gives 0.095 (p = 0.32), so the null does not rest on the fills. October 2025 drops out of the panel entirely because BLS published no CPI that month during the federal shutdown, so real wages cannot be constructed for it. Two-way fixed effects cannot rule out reverse causality, and the industry signs suggest it is present; the causal design (a shift-share instrument) is a separate project and is not claimed here. Government, descriptively one of the most extreme sectors for unfilled postings, has no CES wage series and so cannot enter the industry wage regression at all.

One exclusion should be stated plainly. The original class draft carried a national exhibit purporting to estimate the ghost share of openings over time. That series was constructed rather than measured from source data, and it appears nowhere in this paper, in the rebuild, or in the industry test. Nothing here depends on it.

What would change the conclusions: a friction measure built from posting-level duration data rather than monthly flows; an instrument that moves unfilled openings without moving wages directly; or the registered retest failing on the annual state vintages and division-level data still to come, which would return the headline to the suggestive file it came from. Until one of those exists, the honest statement of the result is the one this paper makes: the phantom is real, it concentrates where predicted, it does not predict wages in general, and since 2022, in the hardest-to-hire states, it does.
