Labor Markets · A Replication

The Tightness Paradox

For a class paper, I found that hiring friction predicts real wages: a coefficient of 6.17, statistically significant, with a paradox attached. Then I rebuilt the study on all fifty states. The effect fell to 0.058 and vanished. What survived the rebuild is a finding about measurement: where unfilled openings actually concentrate, and why they do not mean what tightness is supposed to mean.

1 · The original result

The question came out of the post-pandemic labor market. Openings ran far above unemployment, employers said they could not hire, and a visible share of postings looked like ghost jobs: openings posted with little intent to fill them. The standard tightness measure, openings per unemployed worker, treats every posting as real demand. I wanted to know whether the postings that never convert into hires carry their own signal.

The proxy is simple. Hiring friction is unfilled openings per worker: openings minus hires, scaled by the labor force, lagged one quarter because firms adjust posted wages slowly. The original paper, written for ACCY 512 at Illinois, built a state-by-month panel of the 25 largest states from April 2015 to December 2023 and regressed log real wages on lagged friction with state and month fixed effects, standard errors clustered by state.

The claim (original sample)
Beta = 6.17, p < 0.05

A one-percentage-point rise in the friction proxy was associated with roughly 6 percent higher real wages within states over time.

The paradox
The sign flips

Across states, high-friction states pay less. Within a state over time, rising friction predicted rising wages. That reversal named the paper.

The nulls, reported then too
Two tests came back empty

Friction did not explain the conventional tightness ratio, and the friction-wage link showed no detectable break around the pandemic.

Those two nulls were in the class paper from the start, reported as faithfully as the headline. What the class paper could not do was test its own headline on data it had not selected. The 25 largest states are not a random draw of the U.S. labor market, and a coefficient estimated on them carries that choice inside it. The rebuild was the test.

2 · The rebuild

The rebuild keeps the original design intact and changes one thing: the sample. Same friction formula, same real-wage construction (CES hourly earnings deflated by national CPI into January-2015 dollars), same two-way fixed-effects specification with the same controls, now on all 50 states plus DC, April 2015 to December 2025, 6,528 observations. State JOLTS is what binds the end of the panel.

The first check was whether the rebuild even measures the same thing. On the 2,625 observations where the two panels overlap, labor force, the unemployment rate, hourly wages, and service-sector employment match the original pull exactly. Openings and hires differ slightly, by BLS vintage revisions published since the original download. The inputs agree. Whatever happens to the coefficient is not a data error.

3 · The collapse

The coefficient falls from 6.17 to 0.058. That is a decline of about 99 percent in the point estimate, and the p-value rises from below 0.05 to 0.54. On the full sample, hiring friction does not predict real wages. Even taken at face value, the rebuild's average marginal effect is small: one standard deviation of lagged friction maps to roughly 0.14 percent higher real wages three months later, inside a confidence interval that comfortably includes zero and small negatives.

The one null that replicated. A break test at 2021 finds no structural change (Wald p = 0.26), matching the original paper's Stage 3 null. The original's secondary findings held up. Its headline did not.

The original paper closed by translating its coefficient into a dollar figure, an annual inefficiency tax on employers. That exhibit depended entirely on the 6.17. The rebuild's own back-of-envelope version comes to $11.9B a year with a standard error of $15.3B, which is not an estimate but a range that includes nothing happening. The tax exhibit is cut from this paper. A magnitude built on a coefficient that does not survive its own rebuild is not a magnitude.

A coefficient that falls from 6.17 to 0.06 when the sample doubles was never a fact about the labor market. It was a fact about the sample.

4 · The industry test

A state-level null leaves one place for the story to hide: aggregation. If unfilled openings signal wage pressure only where phantom postings concentrate, the effect should appear at the industry level, and strongest in the industries picked in advance: education and health services, and information. The industry panel is national, 10 industries by 135 months (April 2015 to July 2026), and the friction measure is the direct one: the unfilled share, one minus hires over openings, lagged three months.

The pooled industry regression is also null: a coefficient of 0.0101 on the lagged unfilled share, p = 0.51 with two-way clustered standard errors (N = 1,350). The interaction with the predicted industries is negative and insignificant (p = 0.38), and the two predicted industries are individually null: education and health at p = 0.25, information at p = 0.39.

One number in this section needs handling in the open. Under Driscoll-Kraay standard errors, the pooled industry coefficient carries p = 0.011, which looks like a result. It does not survive contact with the rest of the battery: add industry-specific trends and it falls to p = 0.49; no leave-one-industry-out run is significant; and the per-industry estimates that are significant run mostly negative, the wrong sign for the hypothesis (professional and business services at −0.065, p = 0.0002; financial activities at −0.116, p = 0.0013). A positive pooled coefficient assembled from negative industry coefficients is a weighting artifact. The pre-committed inference and the robustness battery agree: null at the industry level too. The negative signs are themselves informative, and consistent with reverse causality: low-wage industries find openings harder to fill, rather than unfilled openings pushing wages up.

5 · What survives: a measurement finding

The wage claim is gone at both levels. The descriptive fact underneath it is not. Unfilled openings are large, persistent, and concentrated exactly where the original intuition said phantom postings would live.

Where openings go unfilled: mean unfilled share by industry

Computed from the industry panel (industry_panel.csv), means over 135 months per industry. The two clay bars are the industries named in advance, before the test was run.

In education and health services and in professional and business services, about 47 percent of openings do not convert into a hire in the month. In financial activities the share is about 46 percent. Compare total private industry at about 19 percent, trade and transportation at about 9 percent, and two industries where the share is negative: leisure and hospitality and construction, where hires in a month routinely exceed that month's openings because hires fill postings from earlier months.

That spread is the finding. The unfilled share is not a uniform fog over the labor market; it piles up in industries built on credentialed roles, evergreen requisitions, and long hiring pipelines, the places where a posting can stay open without anyone urgently intending to fill it this month. And it can be negative where hiring is fast and churn is high. A measure that behaves this way is not a clean read on demand pressure. Openings minus hires does not behave like tightness: it did not predict wages across states, it did not predict wages across industries, and in the original paper it did not explain the conventional tightness ratio either. Three separate tests point the same way. What JOLTS counts as an opening is a mix of genuine vacancies and postings that were never going to convert on any timeline the wage data can see, and the mix differs by industry in a stable, measurable pattern.

That is a smaller claim than the original paper made. It is also a claim that survived everything thrown at it, including its author re-running the study properly. The paradox dissolves once the measurement is taken seriously: measured tightness looked paradoxical because part of it was never demand.

6 · Method and limits

The rebuild's data choices are documented in its files, and the soft spots are these. State minimum wages before 2019 and education attainment at the sample edges are filled from the nearest observed years, because the cited sources start later; a specification dropping the minimum-wage control gives 0.095 (p = 0.32), so the null does not rest on the fills. October 2025 drops out of the panel entirely because BLS published no CPI that month during the federal shutdown, so real wages cannot be constructed for it. Two-way fixed effects cannot rule out reverse causality, and the industry signs suggest it is present; the causal design (a shift-share instrument) is a separate project and is not claimed here. Government, descriptively one of the most extreme sectors for unfilled postings, has no CES wage series and so cannot enter the industry wage regression at all.

One exclusion should be stated plainly. The original class draft carried a national exhibit purporting to estimate the ghost share of openings over time. That series was constructed rather than measured from source data, and it appears nowhere in this paper, in the rebuild, or in the industry test. Nothing here depends on it.

What would change the conclusion: a friction measure built from posting-level duration data rather than monthly flows, or an instrument that moves unfilled openings without moving wages directly. Until one of those exists, the honest statement of the result is the one this paper makes: the phantom is real, it concentrates where predicted, and it does not predict wages.
