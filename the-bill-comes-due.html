1 · The setup

For two weeks in late October 2026 I traded a paper portfolio through the densest stretch of earnings season, in a university competition run with Interactive Brokers. The rules forced honesty: a fixed book, a hard end date on November 6, and a print almost every day that the book had to survive. Every position carried a file. What the market had priced before the print, what the company actually reported, what the forward numbers did, and what the stock did next.

The competition ends on November 6, the day of the October jobs report. The file does not end with it. The book keeps running on Webull after the competition closes, because the question the season raised is larger than a two-week contest, and the prints keep coming.

The question is simple to state. If beating earnings no longer moves a stock, what exactly is the market paying for?

2 · The model said it first

None of this required the season to discover. Before it started, I built an event study of 167 earnings prints across fourteen names I follow, measuring each print's abnormal reaction against the stock's own history. The point of building it first was discipline: the base rates would be fixed before a single live print could be explained away.

The method started in a classroom. In FIN 559, my quantitative finance course at Illinois, Lecture 15 worked through market responses to earnings announcements as a CAPM event study: estimate each stock's alpha and beta in a window before the event, measure the abnormal return around the print, and split the results by whether expectations were met. A companion lecture ran the same machinery on dividend announcements. I took that method out of the demo dataset and rebuilt it on the names I actually follow, 167 prints deep. The class study asked whether the market responds to earnings. The live version asked a sharper question: what in the print is the market grading?

The base rates were blunt. Of 138 clean beats in the sample, 65 still fell, a rate of 47.1%, and the average beat was worth +1.17%. Of 18 misses, 83.3% fell, at an average of −4.92%. A model that reads only the headline starts the season close to blind: the beat tells you the quarter was fine, and almost nothing about what the stock does next.

The blindness is not an average hiding a pattern. It holds name by name. Apple beat expectations in every one of its last twelve prints in the sample and still averaged a negative reaction. Beating often and being rewarded for it are different things, and the history says the first does not buy the second.

3 · The factor that separated all fourteen

Fourteen of this season's prints were classified against a single question, asked the same way each time: did the forward file improve or deteriorate against the trajectory the market had already priced? The forward file means the guidance, the forward estimates, and the one operating lever the stock was priced on, whether that was a December guide, a growth rate, or an engagement number. The classification was made from the print itself, not from the reaction.

The factor separated all fourteen. Eight deteriorated and all eight were punished. Five improved and all five were rewarded. One control case, a clean beat with the guide held and no surprise in either direction, went flat. The two obvious rivals failed in the same sample.

Species 2 · The slowdown inside the raise

Where the stock was priced on one operating number, that number decided the print. The earnings line was not the lever, so the earnings line did not save it.

The rewarded prints were the mirror image: a guide raised on the lever the stock was priced on, as Eli Lilly did when it won on a revenue-guide raise while cutting its EPS guide, or discipline on spending, as Microsoft showed. And the rewards carry a warning of their own. They fade.

4 · The strategy in action: Intel

One play from the book shows the whole chain, from the class method to the cash. Intel reported after the close on Thursday, October 29. The read beforehand came straight from the two files. Intel's own history in the event study was the one file that said a bad print would keep falling rather than bounce. The options market was charging less for the move than the file said was likely, so the position went in as outright puts rather than shares. And the priced trajectory was the risk: a stock priced for a turnaround story has no cushion when the forward file breaks.

Wed Oct 28 · The ticket
Buy 12 INTC 90 puts, exp. Oct 30

Limit $3.00, staged the night before the print. The strike sat below the $95.53 close, priced off the live chain after a stale quote was caught and discarded.

Thu Oct 29 · The fill and the print
Filled at $2.97 · $3,564 all in

Intel beat the quarter and missed the fourth-quarter guide. The stock fell 25% after hours. The forward file broke, and the headline beat did not matter, exactly as the factor said.

Fri Oct 30 · The exit, written in advance
Sold at $20.00 · +$20,436

The exit was staged before the print: sell at Friday's open, win or lose, because the puts expired that day and holding them meant auto-exercise into a 1,200-share short over the weekend. Sold 6:30 AM, before the open.

The honest footnote: Intel kept falling, to $64.24 by Friday midday, and the puts would have been worth more by then. The exit was not a price call. It was an instrument rule, set when the position was opened, and the model's own file said continuation was the base case. The edge was the guide read. The discipline was leaving on schedule anyway.

5 · The kill zone: guidance down, capex up

The capex version of the rule is where the season's real money sat. All of the AI spending of the last three years now shows in the forward file, and the market reads two numbers together: the outlook, and what the spending is doing to free cash flow. Each combination got a different verdict, and the worst cell was perfect.

File improves · Spending disciplined
Rewarded

Microsoft improved its file and cut capex, and the market treated the cut as discipline. The reward faded within a day, which is a separate finding, but the sign was never in doubt.

File deteriorates · Capex up, free cash flow down
The kill zone: 3 of 3 punished

Cut the outlook while raising the spending and the print gets sold hardest. It tells the market that growth is slowing at exactly the moment the bills get bigger.

File deteriorates · Guide alone
Punished on the guide

Intel and Caterpillar needed no capex story. When the forward file itself breaks, the quarter cannot rescue it.

The recipients get paid
VRT +15.0%

The companies selling the equipment collect the spending without carrying the commitments. Vertiv's print was the season's best, and it is the kill zone read from the other side.

Why is the kill zone punished so much harder than an ordinary guide cut? Because of what the spending has become. The big names funded the build-out from core businesses that carried them for years, and the funding was treated as a choice while free cash flow stayed positive. The commitments are no longer a choice. They are contracts, construction, and capacity that run for years, sized on the assumption that the growth continues. A guide cut says the growth may not continue. The spending continues anyway. That gap, between a falling trajectory and a fixed bill, is what the market is pricing when it sells these prints.

6 · The theme

Put the pieces together and the season has a single rule. The market has decided that sustained growth is the only currency. Positives in the reported quarter go unrewarded when the growth cannot continue, because the price never paid for the quarter. It paid for the trajectory, and the trajectory is what the print is graded on.

A decade of positive free cash flow turning negative is not forgiven on the strength of a cash pile, when the commitments ahead are larger than the pile.

The cash pile deserves one more sentence, because it is the defense every one of these companies offers. Cash on the balance sheet is a stock, a fixed amount. The commitments are a flow that repeats every year the build-out continues. A market that has started grading the forward file does the arithmetic the defense hopes it will skip: the pile covers the commitments only if the core business keeps funding the difference, and the guide is precisely the number that says whether it will. When the guide falls and the spending rises, the arithmetic stops working in public, and the print is where it happens.

Growth is the only currency. The bill for everything else is coming due.

7 · What the prediction markets add: nothing, and one good chart

A natural objection is that prediction markets might price these prints better than a single model. They were checked for all of next week's names, and the answer is no, for a structural reason: the markets that exist price the beat, which is the variable this paper shows does not decide the reaction. They are also nearly empty. The six Polymarket beat books for next week's slate have traded about $6,470 in their entire lives, with bid and ask spreads up to 18 points wide. Shopify and Axon have no market at all, and Airbnb's book has never traded. Kalshi lists no earnings-beat market for any of them, only markets on which words get said on the call.

9 · Method and limits

The honest limits, in the open. The season sample is fourteen classified prints, which is small, and the factor was identified while the season was running, so its perfect record is partly a description of the season that produced it. That is exactly why the test in Section 8 is published before the prints rather than after them. Reaction windows vary across the tape: some are after-hours reads, some are next-session closes, each recorded the same way it was traded. The history is fourteen tickers and 167 prints, deep on the names followed and silent on everything else. The capex cell is three prints inside the hyperscalers, a perfect record on a small count, and it should be weighed that way. The claim that survives all of these limits is the narrow one: in this season, on this evidence, the market graded the forward file and the spending, not the quarter.
