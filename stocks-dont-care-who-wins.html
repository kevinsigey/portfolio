The Midterm Backtest · Elections, Sectors, Energy

Stocks don't care who wins, they're just glad it's over

On Tuesday the country picks a Congress, and by Wednesday the commentary will credit the winner for whatever the market does. I ran the last eight midterms, 1994 to 2022, to check. The winner's identity explains almost none of it. The index moves because the uncertainty ends, sectors move on what the result changes, and oil does not listen to either.

Kevin Sigey · November 2, 2026 · Study: the eight midterms of 1994–2022 · Outcome pricing as of October 31 · Oil as of the October 30 close and the November 1 OPEC+ decision

Executive summary

Eight midterms say that who won is the wrong question to ask the market during election week. The S&P 500 rose on the Wednesday after the vote in seven of eight cycles, 0.48 percent on average, and was higher sixty days later in seven of eight, 4.55 percent on average, under every kind of winner1. The index does not price the winner. It prices the relief that comes when the question is finally answered.

Sectors price something narrower: what the result actually changes. Banks rose on the Wednesday in all four cycles whose outcome was the expected one, a resolution bid that does not care which party supplies it1. Healthcare holds the one sharp exception, a relief trade that exists only when government splits and only when Wednesday's gap confirms it. Section 2 draws the branch record: for each outcome, what the record says moves, and how the evidence grades.

Energy is the exception that proves the rule. In election weeks XLE tracked WTI crude at 0.78 on the Wednesday and 0.95 across the five sessions after the vote1. Once oil's move is taken out, what is left belongs to no party. Oil goes into Tuesday priced on a strait running at 48 percent of prewar flows7, not on the result in Congress.

The priced outcome: a Democratic sweep is the base case

Outcome pricing, October 31, 2026: Polymarket and Kalshi, cross-checked in the author's midterm study1. A priced outcome is a resolution waiting to happen, and the surprise branches are where the sector map earns its keep.

1 · The headline: the market prices the ending

The midterm trade is not a bet on the winner. It is a bet on the ending. Across the eight elections, the Wednesday move averaged +0.48 percent and was positive in seven of eight cycles. Sixty days on, the average was +4.55 percent, again positive in seven of eight1. Grouped by who won, the sixty-day average is positive in every group: +3.4 percent for Republican-favorable cycles, +5.5 percent for the Democratic Congress of 2006, and +5.7 percent for split governments1. Being glad it is over is not a joke about politics. It is the central fact of the sample, and anyone who claims the winner moves the index has to explain away all three groups at once.

Eight midterms, one shape: the index after the vote

SPY indexed to each election-day close. The one down Wednesday, 2022 at −2.06 percent, still finished +8.4 percent at sixty days. The one negative sixty-day window, 2002 at −4.9 percent, came in a winter dominated by the coming Iraq war, not by a verdict on a Republican Senate1.

What the outcome does change is the size of the move, and it works through surprise rather than identity. Grouping the cycles by winner or by expectation explains only about a tenth of Wednesday's variation, which is close to noise1. Surprise cycles simply swung harder. The index's average Wednesday move, up or down, was 1.15 percent in surprise cycles against 0.85 percent in expected ones, and sector moves against the index averaged 1.09 percent against 0.75, about 45 percent larger1. The outcome decides how the story gets told afterwards. Expectations decide how large the move is at the time.

Two honest discounts apply. The sixty-day average leans on 1998 (+13.8 percent, a dot-com winter) and 2022 (+8.4, a bear-market rally). The endpoint also flatters the path, since 2018's window closed +0.5 percent after falling 14.3 percent along the way1. The drift is real, but it is not a free ride.

2 · The sector map: only what a result changes

Sectors do not move on who won. They move on what the win changes: a law that can now pass, a regulator who answers to someone new, or a tail risk that has just died. Tested branch by branch, most of the familiar election trades fail. The record below is the whole of what the evidence supports: three outcome branches, graded in the study's own terms.

Taken raw, the three Wednesdays are a coin flip. The gated version, which buys only when Wednesday's gap lands between +1.0 and +3.5 percent, fired once, in 2018, and paid. The mechanism also has support from the other side. When Democrats took both chambers in 2006, UnitedHealth fell 3.2 percent on the Wednesday and Humana fell 5.9 percent. Looked at across the full sector file underneath those branches, the loudest line is not a Wednesday line. Homebuilders barely move on the Wednesday itself, −0.41 percent on average, then rise over the following sixty days in all five measured cycles, +14.8 percent on average, the largest drift in the panel, and they beat the index over twenty days too, +4.7 points on average. The five cycles cover Republican, Democratic and split governments alike: whatever midterms do to housing, they do it slowly, as the uncertainty ends and the rate path clears, rather than through any winner1. The mirror image sits in energy: producers (XOP) lag the index by 7.1 points over twenty days on average after election weeks, a give-back that section 3 explains. The Wednesday ranking itself is a relief ranking: utilities first (+0.92), solar and materials next, defense and health insurers last. Defensive and rate-sensitive names draw bids when the question closes, while story sectors wait for an actual consequence.

The rest of the map is nulls, and the nulls are the finding. Defense does not move: the defense ETF's Wednesday averaged −0.16 percent across five cycles and was positive once, and the one big defense print, Boeing at +6.5 percent in 2002, came while the country was building up for Iraq1. Clean energy by party does not move: solar names led the 2022 Wednesday, but 2022's surprise was a Democratic Senate hold, so what rose was the sector tied to the surprise, not a party trade1. Small caps get no Wednesday bid: they lagged the S&P 500 on four of six Wednesdays1.

Three trades were refused in advance: the prior study pre-wrote no-trade verdicts for the priced sweep itself (E-3), an unresolved Senate (E-4), and a clean drift (E-5)2. E-6, extending the banks bid into the priced branch, was proposed on the full sample and not adopted. The pattern is in the record, but no trade was built on it1,2.

3 · The exception: oil cares about neither

Energy is the control group. It looks like the most political sector in the market, full of pipelines, leases, and war. In election weeks, though, it trades crude, and the vote accounts for almost none of its move.

XLE and WTI futures overlap in six cycles, from 2002 to 2022. Their correlation was 0.78 on the Wednesday and 0.95 across the five sessions after the vote, counted from the Monday close. When oil's move is stripped out using each cycle's trailing beta, the return left over was positive in all six cycles, between +0.1 and +3.6 percent, under every kind of outcome. That leftover follows no outcome class, and it sits inside estimation noise1. The benchmark for ordinary weeks is a weekly correlation of 0.58 over the same years, so election weeks are more dominated by oil, not less1.

XLE follows WTI through election week, not the count

Wednesday returns, Tuesday close to Wednesday close. Where oil moved hard, as in 2022, energy moved with it. In the quiet cycles it sat still, whatever the count said1.

The story version of events fails on its own names. If Republican strength lifted oil stocks, the surprise cycles would show it, and instead Exxon fell on the Wednesday in all three of them, by 0.82 percent in 1994, 1.37 percent in 1998, and 1.32 percent in 2002. That is three for three with the wrong sign1. The sample's largest energy Wednesday tells the same story: Occidental fell 9.2 percent in 2022 on a day when WTI fell 3.5 percent, which made it an oil day rather than an election day1.

Friday ran the same experiment again in real time. Brent rose 2.4 percent on another tanker attack5 while the S&P 500 fell 1.8 percent, and energy followed crude upward through the falling market, with FANG up 2.7 percent, XOM up 2.2 percent, and XLE up 1.9 percent11. Energy was following the barrel, not the polls.

What oil is pricing instead is a blockade. Flows through Hormuz ran at 48 percent of their prewar level on Kpler's seven-day average to October 267, and the EIA assumes 4.5 million barrels a day shut in through the fourth quarter8. OPEC+ produced about 4.2 million barrels a day below its targets in September10 and held its December targets flat on Sunday, the third hold in a row9. A group producing that far below its own ceiling cannot add supply simply by raising the ceiling. The supply decision that matters is the November 29 ministerial.

The premium bleeds on words, at $2 to $3 per mediation headline4, and Brent put open interest sits at a record5, which means the largest hedge in the market pays out on a peace deal. The one political catalyst this week is a decision rather than a result: CENTCOM's commander briefed the President on Iran options on Thursday, and no final decision had been made as of Saturday15,5. Every reader pays for this whether they trade it or not, because US diesel set a record at $6.53 a gallon in September16.

If oil is the variable, the vehicle question is purity. XLE is an index, and an index absorbs: its largest holdings are integrated majors whose refining, chemicals and marketing earnings do not track the barrel, plus refiners and pipelines whose profits can rise when crude falls17. At a 0.31 beta, 10 percent in crude is roughly 3 percent in XLE, and over six months to October 26 it ranked tenth of sixteen3. XOP holds producers only, in roughly equal weights18, so it carries more crude per dollar and reaches 0.71. The top of the ranking is a single name3.

Occidental is built like crude. Correlation 0.74 to WTI, beta 0.50, purity rank one of sixteen. On days WTI moved more than 2 percent, OXY captured 58 percent of up moves and 49 percent of down moves; XLE managed 37 and 263. The purity is structural. Occidental is one of the largest independent producers in the Permian, and a bigger one since the $12 billion CrownRock acquisition in 2024, producing about 1.43 million barrels of oil equivalent a day19. Its shareholder base matches: Berkshire Hathaway owns about 27 percent of the common stock, roughly $8.5 billion of 8 percent preferred stock, and warrants on 83.9 million shares at $59.5920. Berkshire also bought its chemicals arm, OxyChem, for $9.7 billion in January21, and the company is aiming a strong second quarter, with $5.1 billion of operating cash flow and about $3 billion of free cash flow, at debt of $11.8 billion against a $10 billion target19.

Purity is not protection. OXY carries about 23 percent residual volatility, which is the part of its ride that is not oil, against 16.9 percent for XLE, and an index cannot gap on one company's guidance the way a single stock can. XLE is ballast, and that is a use rather than an insult. Occidental's third-quarter results land on Monday, November 919, so anyone who owns it for an oil view also owns the barrel, a balance sheet in the middle of repair, and an earnings print. The ordering for Tuesday is settled, and it points to the strait rather than the count.

4 · Limits, and what would change this

The sample is eight elections. Everything above should be read at that size. Sector funds exist only from 2002, so sector cells run on six cycles or fewer, several branches rest on one clean cycle plus a mechanism, and the grades in section 2 say which is which. Earnings season overlaps every midterm: the sample's biggest single 'election mover,' Enphase, down 22.8 percent on the Wednesday of 2014, was reporting its own earnings that day1. The sixty-day drift, homebuilders included, runs into year-end seasonality, which this design cannot separate from the election itself.

The study also writes down in advance what would prove it wrong, and this week tests two of those conditions live. A negative bank Wednesday on a confirmed, priced Democratic sweep breaks the four-for-four resolution bid. An in-band healthcare gap on a confirmed split that is down through Friday makes E-1 one-for-two and kills the spec. A surprise branch printing sector dispersion below the expected-cycle norm of about 0.75 kills the amplitude claim1. Two negative sixty-day windows in split or expected cells would demote the drift to a 1998-to-2022 regime artifact.

5 · The conclusion: an ordinary loud week

This week is loud because several unrelated things are happening at once. Congress is chosen on Tuesday. The December oil contract gives way to January on Monday. A strike decision is still open in Washington, briefed on Thursday and unmade as of Saturday15,5, and a jobs report lands on Friday. Any one of these can move a session, and together they make the week sound more dramatic than it is.

The record puts the week in proportion. The session the eight cycles actually grade is Wednesday's close, and what it graded was the ending rather than the winner, positive in seven of eight cycles under every kind of government1. Monday's screens will show oil down about four dollars that nobody sold, because December expired at $117.64 and January opens at $113.435. That is arithmetic, not news. The energy date with a real decision attached is November 29, when OPEC+ next sets supply9; until then the premium moves on words from a strait, at $2 to $3 a headline4, rather than on anything Congress does.

None of this makes the week quiet, and the record does not claim otherwise. It makes a narrower point. Fear is the loudest part of an election week and also the shortest-lived, because once the question closes, the surprise is absorbed and the market goes back to pricing consequences, which is what it was pricing all along. By Wednesday night the winner will be credited with whatever the market does. The eight cycles suggest the credit is misaddressed, and that it changes nothing about what the market does next. This is not a recommendation. It is a record: eight elections, read the same way, with the failures left in.

Sources and method.

The midterm study (source 1) covers the eight elections of 1994 to 2022; all returns are adjusted-close total returns. The reaction window is Wednesday's close against Tuesday's; the five, twenty and sixty day windows run from the Monday close before the vote; sector returns are also shown against the S&P 500. Every cut is run twice, by who won and by whether the result was the priced one, because prices respond to the surprise, not to the label. Section 2's 2026 prices are the study's October 31 snapshot (source 1).

The full model, data, and code behind sections 1 to 3 are public: github.com/kevinsigey/midterm-elections-model. The branch record in section 2 is drawn from the study's sector file, computed for this paper across every vehicle that file supports: seventeen funds, summarized at their means; the cycle-by-cycle detail is in the repository. Communications (XLC) is absent because the fund launched in 2018 and has no midterm history in the panel; nothing was estimated to fill its place.

For energy, the same study supplies the election-week decomposition (source 1). The five-session correlation of 0.95 is counted from the Monday close, the study's window convention; from the Tuesday close the same window gives 0.83, and the conclusion is unchanged. The lab (source 3) regresses sixteen energy vehicles on WTI over six months of daily log returns to October 26, 2026; purity is R squared times the absolute beta. The monitor (source 4) logs how the premium responded to individual headlines, and those entries are observations, not estimates.
