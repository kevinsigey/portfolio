The Gray Area: Using game theory to predict the AI boom

Twelve players are building, funding, permitting and policing the AI build-out at the same time. The builders want infinite growth, but the power the grid can supply is very much finite, and the people who ration it (utilities, voters, mayors, commissions) are already saying no, or saying yes at full price. I modelled the whole thing as a game, with every player, every lever and 24,576 possible worlds, and the punchline is that today isn't one of the stable ones. My call is that nobody wins outright: it'll be a stalemate on the grid's terms until new information changes the chessboard. Below is my prediction, the game behind it, the supporting evidence, and the dates I think the game will begin to change.

55%Stalemate
20%Boom
25%Crash

These odds are my judgment rather than a model output, because the model tells you which worlds can survive, not how likely each one is or how fast we get there.

The short version. Most of the probability sits in the gray area, where we stay roughly where we are until something forces a move. If we do leave, crash is the likelier exit, while boom is the long shot because the model gives it nothing to stand on: a boom where the grid stops charging full cost never holds. The gray area isn't calm either, since the strong builders keep growing on the grid's terms while the weak ones, small firms and leveraged neoclouds whose debt costs more than their revenue, fold or get bought.

The game

The game: why the middle holds, and why it won't forever

Twelve players, fifteen levers, 24,576 possible worlds. That's 32,768 combinations minus the 8,192 where neoclouds borrow money nobody is lending them. Every player scores each lever from −3 to +3 based on what it has at stake, and once every world runs through four stability tests the headline is simple: today isn't stable.

24,576possible worlds
6,735stable once any answer counts (GMR, SMR)
1,090stable on believable threats alone (SEQ)
1stable with no threats needed (Nash)
  • Stable: nobody has a move worth making, or every move meets a believable answer.
  • Fragile: held in place partly by bluffs.
  • Unstable: someone can move, and nothing stops them.

Today is unstable. Four players would gain by moving (OpenAI, the SEC, the Fed and the grid), and while three of those moves can be answered, the grid's can't. Charging data centres full cost for their power, which AEP Ohio and Virginia already do, is worth +3 to the grid, and no single reply costs it more than 1.

Flip gatekeeping on and you get the gray area. It's fragile: every tempting move now has an answer, but the SEC's and the Fed's answers are bluffs. That's the stalemate. The build-out keeps going on the grid's terms, slower, pricier and with strings attached, until new information calls a bluff.

Gatekeeping never switches back off either. It's on in every stable world the model finds (54–58% if players can gang up on a reply).

How to read the four tests: Nash, GMR, SMR and SEQ, and what GMCR is
Nash: nobody wants to move

Nobody at the table has any move that pays, even before anyone answers. The strictest test. Only 1 world in 24,576 passes it.

GMR: every gain gets answered

Someone might gain by moving, but another player can answer in a way that leaves the mover no better off. If every tempting move has an answer like that, the world holds.

SMR: even the comeback fails

Like GMR, except the mover also gets to answer the answer. If they still cannot come out ahead, the world holds.

SEQ: the threat has to be believable

Like GMR, except the answer only counts if the player making it would actually want to make it. Empty threats do not hold a world together.

What GMCR is. The Graph Model for Conflict Resolution (Fang, Hipel & Kilgour, 1993) is a way of solving conflicts with many decision-makers, vetoes and sanctions, where no honest dollar payoff exists for a ballot measure or a permit. It needs only three things: who can move, what their options are, and how they rank the outcomes. Every possible combination of options is a state. A state is an equilibrium under a test when no player can do better by leaving it, once the replies that test allows are counted. That is the whole engine behind the numbers above: 12 players, 15 options, 24,576 feasible states, each one checked under all four tests.

How the scores work. Classic GMCR ranks outcomes in strict order, best to worst, for each player. This model scores the documented preference behind each ranking on a scale from −3 to +3 for each lever and adds the scores. The total is a straight-line stand-in for the ranking. It keeps the order the evidence supports, and it makes the size of a gain or a loss visible. The main result does not depend on any one score. Gatekeeping gains 3 and can lose at most 1 to any single reply. That gap is wider than any single weight in the table. Two sets of weights are settings, not measured values: the cross-lab rivalry weights, −2 on each other's IPOs and −1 on each other's commitments, set from the comparable-pricing evidence, and the weights for the seven players added beyond the core five. They are documented analyst settings, and the sensitivity checks behind the robust worlds are run across them.

What everyone can see. The model gives every player the same board. Every lever, and every other player's position, is visible to all twelve. Real life is not like that. Debt sits in vehicles that do not report in full. Rate cases are filed on schedules the builders do not control. Commitments surface in filings months after they are signed. That gap between the board in the model and the board in real life is the point. The middle holds while the information is incomplete. It moves when new filings, new tariffs or new prices change what everyone can see.

The three paths

What has to happen, each way

The middle holds until new information shakes it. If something does break, crash is the likelier exit, because nobody chooses a funding strike but once one starts it sticks, and blanket politics is only two moves away. Boom gets no help from the model. Here's what to watch, each way:

Stalemate55%

The crack arrives slowly and on the grid's terms, so building continues, but easy growth and easy financing don't.

  • Gatekeeping spreads: tariffs at 85% of contracted cost, state by state
  • Capex stays high, growth stops: digestion language gets rewarded
  • The weak get margined out: credit costs more than neocloud revenue can carry, so they fold or sell
  • Stalled campuses sell at a discount instead of new ones being announced

Think shale after 2014, which didn't die but consolidated on the lenders' terms (more on that below).

Boom20%

The decision makers choose speed, so utilities and commissions connect and condition instead of blocking, and everything else holds at once.

  • Permits come with conditions, not bans
  • Capex guidance holds or rises; any trim is blamed on supply, not demand
  • Anthropic prices near talk and holds; Nvidia confirms demand on Nov 17
  • Private credit keeps refinancing the neocloud layer at investment grade spreads
Crash25%

The crack arrives fast, through money or blanket politics, and history says it takes a pair of triggers rather than one bad day.

  • Funding strike: an SPV or neocloud refinancing fails and spreads gap wider
  • Demand miss: Nvidia guides down, or a hyperscaler cuts capex citing demand
  • A lab cannot meet its commitments: the IMF's triple whammy
  • Politics turns blanket: bans replace conditions

Three build-outs, one pattern

Every build-out like this has the same three parts: money lent ahead of demand, a leveraged weak link, and a credit shock. Telecom took the crash path and shale took the stalemate path. AI already has the first two, and the third would be the funding strike, world 3b in the game.

The call

So where does that leave things?

The stalemate holds until new information shakes it. The builders want infinite growth and the grid is finite, so nobody wins outright, and credit decides who survives the wait. I put it at 55% stalemate, 20% boom and 25% crash, and I'll be graded on the dates above, starting with Nvidia on Nov 17 and Anthropic's first trading days.
